Motor accident compensation (s 166 MV Act) in the Supreme Court of India, 2021–2026

Rishika Bairagi (Sec B) · Content analysis of 7 Supreme Court judgments · Dataset: data.csv / data.xlsx

1. What we studied

When a person is injured or killed in a road accident, a claim for compensation is filed under s 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, usually against the owner and the insurer of the vehicle. We read 7 reasoned Supreme Court judgments on this topic decided between 1 September 2021 and 31 August 2026 and coded each one for its year, bench, outcome, winner, provision, issue, holding and leading precedent.

2. The numbers

Every ordinary appeal in the set came to the Court as a civil appeal and was decided by a bench of one to four judges (most often two). The Court has not relied on one dominant precedent: each judgment leans on a different earlier case (Sunita, Mukund Dewangan, Dulcina Fernandes, Pran Sukh, Gyan Prakash), which suggests the law in this area is being built case by case on separate questions.

3. How the Court’s approach developed

Reading the holdings in date order, three phases appear.

2022: proof and liability between owner and insurer. The Court corrected a High Court that had cut an award by applying strict evidentiary standards. It held that the test is preponderance of probabilities and that a salary certificate backed by oral evidence is good proof of income (Rajwati). The same year it dealt with who must pay between the respondents under s 149 (V. Renganathan).

2024–2025: narrowing technical defences and protecting the victim. The Court upheld Mukund Dewangan and held that an LMV licence holder may drive a transport vehicle up to 7,500 kg without a separate endorsement (Bajaj Allianz v. Rambha Devi), which removes a common insurer defence. It then held that a pillion rider was not contributorily negligent because the scooter driver had only a learner’s licence, and it ordered full compensation with interest (Srikrishna Kanta Singh). It also required insurers to pay enhanced compensation directly to the claimant’s bank account within six weeks (Parminder Singh).

2025–2026: fixing the system, not just the case. The Court began issuing general directions. It addressed large sums lying undisbursed with Tribunals (In Re: Compensation Amounts Deposited with MACTs) and held that the police accident information report may be treated as a claim application (In Re: Phalodi Accident).

Overall trend: in this sample the Court moves from deciding proof and liability disputes in individual appeals, towards narrowing insurer defences, and then towards procedural directions meant to get money to victims faster. The pattern is consistent with a victim-friendly, beneficial reading of the Act.

5. Limits

7 judgments are a very small sample and were selected by us, so these are observations about this set, not statistics about every motor accident case in the Supreme Court. Year counts in particular can change a lot if a few more judgments are added. Coding involved our own reading of each judgment, and a different reader might classify some entries (for example “Who won”) differently.
